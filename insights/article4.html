The $23,000 Cycle and the Coverage Gap Nobody Closes

A single IVF cycle averages $23,474 in the United States. The drugs just got cheaper. The procedure did not — and the coverage system is built to leave most patients paying for it themselves.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Jaclyn Glass did everything the system told her to do. She found an employer whose insurance was subject to a state mandate, used it to have her son in 2022, then changed jobs to a company that offered fertility benefits through Carrot Fertility and had a daughter in 2024. Even with coverage twice over, she and her husband estimate they paid about $8,000 out of pocket across two rounds. Without it, the bill would have been measured in tens of thousands. Her story is the good version. Most patients never get the first part of it.

The price is the easy part to state. A single IVF cycle averages $23,474 in the United States, according to FertilityIQ figures cited across 2026 coverage, and most patients need two to three cycles — $40,000 to $75,000 or more before medications, genetic testing, and monitoring costs that many plans do not cover. Medications alone run $3,000 to $8,000 per cycle depending on protocol. The American Society for Reproductive Medicine puts the average single-cycle figure at $23,474. Carrot Fertility's own accounting puts the typical patient undergoing two to three cycles at roughly $50,000 out of pocket. These are not the prices of a luxury good. They are the prices of a medical procedure that a large share of patients pay for the way they would pay for a car — in cash, in installments, or not at all.

The policy response, two years into a presidential promise that the government or insurers would pay for IVF, has narrowed to the cheapest part of the bill. An executive order in February 2025 directed agencies to produce recommendations within 90 days; it changed no law, required no coverage, and appropriated no money. In October 2025 the White House announced an agreement with EMD Serono to sell three fertility medications at most-favored-nation pricing through a federal website, with the company saying patients using all three therapies in a typical protocol would see an 84 percent discount off list prices. TrumpRx.gov went live in early February 2026 listing Gonal-F — list price above $900 — at $168. It is a real discount on a real drug. It is also a discount on the smallest part of the invoice: egg retrieval, anesthesia, monitoring ultrasounds, embryology laboratory work, embryo transfer, genetic testing, and storage fees are untouched by the drug agreement, and patients whose protocols use Follistim or Menopur rather than the three covered drugs need another source entirely.

The coverage map underneath the headlines is where the gap actually lives. As of 2026, 15 states plus Washington, D.C. require insurance plans to cover IVF specifically, and 25 states plus D.C. require some form of fertility coverage. California's SB 729, the largest single change of 2026, requires large group plans to cover IVF with up to three egg retrievals and unlimited embryo transfers, with LGBTQ+ patients explicitly included. Georgia, Florida, and Nevada added fertility-preservation coverage for cancer patients in 2025–2026, the fastest-growing mandate type. But mandates do not apply to self-funded employer plans, which are governed by federal ERISA law rather than state insurance law — and about 67 percent of workers with employer-sponsored insurance are in self-funded plans. The mandates cover the minority. The majority's coverage depends on the employer, and only about a quarter of employers report offering IVF coverage at all.

Employers are moving, just not fast enough to close the gap. Mercer finds that about half of U.S. employers with at least 500 workers now offer IVF coverage, up from 22 percent in 2019. A 2026 survey by the International Foundation of Employee Benefit Plans found 30 percent of employers cover IVF, up from 14 percent a decade ago, and egg-freezing coverage has risen from 2 percent to 18 percent of employers over the same period. In May 2026 the Departments of Labor, Health and Human Services, and Treasury proposed a rule that would let employers offer standalone fertility benefits as excepted benefits, with a lifetime cap of up to $120,000 and elective egg freezing excluded. KFF's analysis found the guidance creates no new benefit category, no mandate, and no federal subsidy. Sean Tipton, chief advocacy officer for the American Society for Reproductive Medicine, put the practical advice bluntly: “Fertility treatments are time sensitive. Do not delay your treatment thinking there's going to be a benefit from this policy anytime soon.”

The research on what cheaper treatment would actually buy is the most striking number in this whole picture. A study presented at the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology's 2026 meeting, drawing on registry data from 22 countries covering more than 95 percent of assisted reproduction worldwide, found that halving what patients pay out of pocket is associated with a 2.67-fold increase in live births achieved through assisted reproduction. The same cycle that costs roughly $23,000 in the United States costs less than a third of that in Spain or the Czech Republic. The United States is not paying more for better outcomes. It is paying more for fewer of them.

Here is the counterargument, and it is the one the industry itself makes. IVF is expensive because it is labor-intensive, low-volume, highly skilled work — embryologists, anesthesiologists, and laboratory infrastructure that cannot be scaled the way a drug can. A mandate that forced every plan to cover unlimited cycles would raise premiums for everyone, including the majority who will never need the benefit, and the history of state mandates shows the predictable result: narrow eligibility, cycle caps, and waiting periods that ration by paperwork instead of price. The drug discount, for all its limits, is at least a discount that exists, and the employer-benefit pathway at least lets companies compete for talent by offering it. A system that rations by employer generosity is unfair. A system that rations by law is also a rationing system — it just hides the queue.

The implication for a patient is unromantic and urgent. Check the state mandate, then check whether it applies to your plan — if your employer self-funds, it almost certainly does not. Check the employer's benefit before the job, not after the diagnosis, because half of large employers now cover IVF and the other half do not, and the difference is worth tens of thousands. Use the drug discount where it applies, and do not mistake it for coverage of the procedure. And treat time as the scarcest input: the policy debate will still be running in a year, and the biology will not wait for it. The $23,000 cycle is not the problem. The problem is a system that decided, quietly and in pieces, that most people should pay it themselves.
