Egg Freezing, Priced by the Year: Storage Fees Are the Bill That Never Ends

One retrieval cycle costs $10,000 to $20,000. The storage bill that follows is $500 to $1,200 a year — and it is the only line item on the invoice that never ends.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The quote arrived in a patient portal, itemised the way a car lease is itemised. One egg-freezing cycle: $10,000 to $20,000, all in. Medication: $3,000 to $7,000. And then, near the bottom, the line that reads like an afterthought: storage, $500 to $1,200 a year. The patient in question was 34, a marketing director with a calendar that did not have a baby in it for at least three more years. She did the arithmetic the clinic did not do for her. At $800 a year, ten years of storage is $8,000. At $1,200, it is $12,000. The storage bill is the only line on the invoice that never ends, and it is the one nobody talks about when they talk about the price of freezing eggs.

That is the strange economics of egg freezing. The headline number — the cycle — is large and finite. The storage fee is small and permanent. A patient who freezes at 32 and uses the eggs at 40 will pay for eight years of storage before the eggs do anything at all. If she freezes at 32 and never uses them, she will have paid for a decade of storage for a claim on a future she did not take up. The fee is not a rounding error. Over the realistic holding period it is often the second-largest cost in the entire exercise, behind only the retrieval itself.

The cycle is the expensive part, and it is not one cycle

The first bill is the retrieval cycle, and it is the one clinics advertise. A single cycle, all in, typically costs $10,000 to $20,000, according to the fertility pricing guides published by Neena Health and ConceiveGuide in 2026, and the Pacific Fertility Center of Los Angeles puts the same range at $10,000 to $20,000 for a single cycle, $21,000 for two and $30,000 for three, before medication. Medication is a separate line and it is not small: $3,000 to $7,000 per cycle, depending on the protocol and the dose, with the higher end applying to patients who need more stimulation to produce enough eggs. The cycle fee itself — monitoring, ultrasound, anaesthesia, the laboratory work — generally runs $6,000 to $15,000 before drugs are added.

And a single cycle is rarely the plan. The clinical target for a reasonable chance of a live birth is roughly 20 mature eggs, and most patients do not get 20 mature eggs in one retrieval. A 2025 retrospective study cited by ConceiveGuide found that patients who froze 20 or more eggs before age 36 had a 70 to 80 percent cumulative live birth rate. Getting there usually takes two or three cycles. ConceiveGuide's working total for a typical patient is $20,000 to $50,000 or more, including medication and several years of storage. The per-cycle price is the advertised number. The per-patient price is the real one.

Clinics know this, which is why multi-cycle packages exist. CNY Fertility, the low-cost chain, advertises egg freezing at $4,997 for the first cycle and $3,995 for additional cycles — one of the lowest sticker prices in the market, before medication and storage. The Pacific Fertility Center of Los Angeles prices its three-cycle package at $30,000, against $11,000 for a single cycle. The packages are a genuine saving for patients who will need more than one round, and they are also a signal: the clinic expects you back. Budget for two cycles and be pleasantly surprised if one is enough.

The storage annuity

Storage is where the bill changes character. It is priced like a subscription, not like a medical procedure, and it is priced by the year because the eggs are stored by the year. Neena Health puts annual storage at $500 to $1,200. ConceiveGuide puts it at $500 to $1,000 a year. The Pacific Fertility Center charges $700 a year. Kindbody, the clinic network, charges $600 a year for the first year. The range is narrow and the direction is one way: it does not fall.

Do the maths on a realistic holding period. A patient freezes at 33 and returns at 39. Six years at $800 a year is $4,800. At $1,200 a year it is $7,200. Freeze at 30 and return at 40 and the bill is $8,000 to $12,000 — the price of a second retrieval cycle, paid in annual instalments for the privilege of keeping what you already paid to produce. Some clinics include the first year of storage in the cycle price. After that, the meter runs. A patient who freezes at 28, the age at which fertility clinics now routinely market the procedure to women in their twenties, and does not return until 40, will have paid twelve years of storage before the eggs are ever used. At $1,000 a year that is $12,000 — more than the cost of the retrieval cycle that produced them.

The storage fee also has a quality that the cycle fee does not: it is recurring, and it is easy to forget. Clinics store eggs in liquid nitrogen at minus 196 degrees Celsius, in tanks that are monitored continuously. The fee covers the nitrogen, the monitoring, the facility and the liability. It is not, in the main, a profit centre. But it is a bill that arrives every year, and the decision it forces every year — keep paying, or stop — is a decision about a future the patient has not yet chosen. The patients who freeze and never return are not rare. They are the storage industry's most reliable customers.

The thaw is not free either

Storage is not the last bill. When the eggs are used, there is a thaw fee, a fertilisation fee, embryo culture, and a transfer — and those procedures are priced separately from the original cycle. ConceiveGuide puts the cost of using frozen eggs, including thawing, fertilisation and transfer, at $4,000 to $10,000 or more. Add genetic testing of the resulting embryos, which many clinics recommend for patients using frozen eggs at an older age, at $3,000 to $5,000, and the cost of turning stored eggs into a pregnancy can approach the cost of a fresh IVF cycle. The eggs were frozen to buy time. The thaw buys the pregnancy, and it is priced accordingly.

There is also the question of what happens to eggs that are never used. Clinics offer donation to research, donation to another patient, or disposal. None of those options refunds the storage fees. The economics of egg freezing are the economics of insurance: you pay for a claim you hope to make, and the premium is the price of the option, not of the outcome.

Who pays, and why that is changing

For most of its history, egg freezing was an out-of-pocket expense, and for most patients it still is. That is changing at the edges. Employer coverage of egg freezing has grown from 2 percent of employers a decade ago to 18 percent in 2026, according to the International Foundation of Employee Benefit Plans survey cited in our earlier reporting on the IVF coverage gap. The employers offering it are, for the most part, large technology and professional-services firms — the same firms whose employees are the likeliest to freeze. The benefit is real, and it is also narrow: it reaches the employees of companies large enough to self-insure and generous enough to include fertility in the plan.

The fertility-benefits companies are part of the same shift. Carrot Fertility and Progyny sell fertility benefits to employers as a package, and egg freezing is typically the first benefit in the package, because it is the one the youngest employees use. Cofertility, a newer entrant, offers a model in which a patient can freeze eggs for free by donating half of the retrieved eggs to another patient — a price of zero on the cycle, in exchange for eggs. The model is clever and it is also a reminder of how expensive the standard route is: when the alternative is giving away half your eggs, the market price of a cycle is doing a lot of work.

The United Kingdom offers a useful comparison. The Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority puts the cost of egg collection and freezing at £3,350, with medication at £500 to £1,500 and storage at £125 to £350 a year — storage roughly a third of the American price. The difference is regulation and scale, not biology. American storage is priced by private clinics in a private market. British storage is priced inside a regulated system. The gap is a measure of what an unregulated market charges for a cold tank.

The counterargument: it is still the cheapest insurance in fertility

None of this makes egg freezing a bad decision. It makes it a decision that should be priced honestly. The honest price of freezing eggs at 33 and using them at 39 is not $15,000. It is $15,000 for the cycles, $5,000 to $7,000 for six years of storage, and $4,000 to $10,000 to use them — $24,000 to $32,000 in total, before genetic testing, and more if a second transfer is needed. That is still, in most cases, less than a fresh IVF cycle at 40 using eggs that are six years older, and it is far less than the cost of donor eggs, which run $35,000 to $80,000 all in. The storage fee is the bill that never ends. It is also, for the patient who needs it, the cheapest year-by-year purchase in fertility medicine: roughly $800 a year to hold the age of your eggs still while the rest of your life moves forward.

The mistake is not freezing. The mistake is budgeting for the cycle and forgetting the annuity. Ask the clinic for the storage fee in writing, for the annual increase, for what happens if you stop paying, and for the cost of the thaw before you sign. The retrieval is a procedure. The storage is a subscription. Price both.
