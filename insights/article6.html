How Long an IVF Cycle Actually Takes, Week by Week

A fresh cycle runs about four weeks from the first injection to the pregnancy test. A freeze-all cycle with genetic testing runs eight to ten. The calendar is the part of IVF nobody quotes you.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The nurse said four weeks. That was the number the clinic gave the couple at the first consultation: one fresh IVF cycle, four weeks from the first injection to the pregnancy test. It was a true number, and it was not the number that mattered. By the time the blood tests, the genetic screening, the insurance authorisation and the calendar of the embryology lab were done, they were in month three. The injections took twelve days. The waiting took the rest. Anyone who has done IVF will tell you the same thing: the medical part is measured in weeks, and the whole thing is measured in months.

The gap between the two numbers is the gap between a protocol and a process. A protocol is a sequence of medical steps with known durations. A process is a protocol plus a clinic's schedule, a laboratory's queue, an insurer's paperwork and a patient's body, which does not always do what the protocol asks on the day the protocol asks it. The four-week figure is real. It is also the shortest version of the story, told by people who sell the procedure.

The four-week cycle, step by step

For a fresh cycle, the medical timeline is short. Dr. Lucky Sekhon, a reproductive endocrinologist at RMA of New York, puts it at about four weeks from the first day of stimulation to the pregnancy test. Stimulation — daily injections to grow multiple follicles — lasts 8 to 14 days, according to the American Society for Reproductive Medicine. The trigger injection is given when the follicles are ready, and the retrieval happens about 36 hours later. Fertilisation happens the same day. If the plan is a fresh transfer, the embryo goes back three to five days after retrieval, and the pregnancy test follows 9 to 14 days after the transfer. Four weeks, start to finish, if everything goes to plan.

It is worth pausing on the word "if". Stimulation can be extended by several days if the follicles are slow to respond. The retrieval can be cancelled if too few follicles develop, or converted to a freeze-all cycle if the hormone levels run too high. The fresh transfer can be cancelled if the uterine lining is not ready. Each of those is a normal, common event in IVF, and each of them adds days or weeks to a timeline that was quoted as four. A cycle that is quoted in weeks is lived in the conditional tense.

The freeze-all cycle: eight to ten weeks

Many clinics no longer do fresh transfers at all. The freeze-all approach — retrieve, fertilise, freeze the embryos, test them, and transfer in a later cycle — has become the default at a growing number of practices, because it allows genetic testing and because it separates the stimulation from the transfer, which improves the uterine environment. It also roughly doubles the timeline. Dr. Sekhon puts a freeze-all cycle with preimplantation genetic testing at eight to ten weeks from the first injection to the pregnancy test.

The extra weeks are mostly waiting. After the retrieval, the embryos are cultured to the blastocyst stage, five to seven days after fertilisation. A biopsy is taken and sent for genetic testing, and the results take roughly a week and a half to two weeks. Then the transfer cycle begins: three to four weeks of preparation to build the uterine lining, either with medication or in a natural cycle, before the frozen embryo is thawed and transferred. The pregnancy test follows nine to fourteen days after that. The medical work is the same as in a fresh cycle. The calendar is twice as long because the laboratory and the body each need their own month.

There is a further wrinkle that clinics rarely mention in the first consultation. A frozen embryo transfer cycle is itself a cycle, with its own monitoring appointments, its own blood tests and its own schedule. The Pacific Reproductive Center puts a frozen embryo transfer cycle at two to three weeks of preparation. Shady Grove Fertility, one of the largest clinic networks in the country, notes that the transfer cycle's timing depends on the patient's own menstrual cycle, which means the start date is not chosen by the clinic or the patient but by the body. A cycle that is supposed to start on a Monday starts when it starts.

Before the first injection: two to six weeks

The weeks before the cycle begins are the ones nobody counts. Dr. Sekhon notes that testing and planning before a cycle typically add two to six weeks. The standard workup — blood tests for hormone levels, screening for infectious disease, a semen analysis, genetic carrier screening, an ultrasound to count the antral follicles — takes a week or two of appointments. The results take another week. Then there is the protocol decision, the medication order, and the insurance authorisation, which in the states without an IVF mandate can take two to four weeks and can be denied and appealed. The Fertility Institute of San Diego puts the full journey, from the first consultation to the pregnancy test, at three to four months for a typical patient.

Some of that time is the patient's own doing. Many clinics ask patients to take birth control pills for two to four weeks before stimulation, to synchronise the follicles and to schedule the retrieval. The pills are not a medical necessity in every case; they are a scheduling tool, used to batch patients so the embryology laboratory can handle them in cohorts. The patient experiences it as a delay. The clinic experiences it as a queue. Both are correct.

The counterargument: the waiting is not waste

It is tempting to read the timeline as a defect — a process that could be faster if the system were better organised. That is half true. The other half is that much of the waiting is the medicine. Genetic testing takes two weeks because the laboratory needs two weeks. The uterine lining takes three to four weeks to prepare because the endometrium grows at its own rate. A freeze-all cycle is longer than a fresh cycle, but the evidence from large clinic networks is that it produces better outcomes for many patients, precisely because it does not rush the transfer. Speed is not the variable to optimise. The variable to optimise is the chance of a live birth per cycle, and the calendar is the price of that chance.

The honest version of the timeline, then, is not four weeks. It is four weeks of medical steps inside eight to ten weeks of a freeze-all cycle inside three to four months of a full process, with the possibility of a second cycle if the first does not work. A patient who is told that at the first consultation is a patient who can plan. A patient who is told four weeks and is still waiting in month four is a patient who has lost trust in the clinic, and trust is the one thing in IVF that cannot be bought back.

Ask for the timeline in writing, with the testing phase, the authorisation phase and the transfer phase shown separately. Ask whether the clinic does fresh or frozen transfers as its default, and why. Ask what happens if the cycle is cancelled mid-stimulation, and how long the restart takes. The answers are not a guarantee. They are the difference between a calendar you can live with and a calendar that lives with you.
