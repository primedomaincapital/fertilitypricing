The Donor Egg Premium: What Third-Party Reproduction Adds to the Price

A frozen donor-egg cohort costs $18,000 before a single medical procedure. A fresh cycle through an agency runs $35,000 to $80,000 all-in. The premium buys the highest success rate in fertility medicine — and the most complicated invoice.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The consultation went well until the price came up. The patient was 41, and the doctor was kind and direct: her own eggs gave her a low chance, and the clinic recommended donor eggs. The quote was $45,000, before medication and before genetic testing. She had been quoted $23,000 for a cycle with her own eggs. The difference — $22,000, roughly double — was the donor egg premium, and nobody in the room could explain, line by line, what it bought. It bought the highest success rate in fertility medicine. It also bought the most complicated invoice in fertility medicine, and the two facts are related.

Donor-egg IVF is the most expensive form of IVF and the most successful. The eggs come from a donor in her twenties, which means the success rate is the success rate of a 25-year-old, not of a 41-year-old. The premium is the price of that substitution. What is less well understood is how the premium is assembled — and how much of it is negotiable, avoidable, or simply opaque.

The two ways to buy donor eggs

There are two routes, and they are priced like two different products. The first is a fresh cycle through a donor agency. The agency recruits and screens the donor, the clinic synchronises the donor's cycle with the recipient's, and the eggs are retrieved fresh and fertilised the same day. The Fertility Link, a pricing guide published in 2026, puts the all-in cost of a fresh agency cycle at $35,000 to $80,000 in the United States, and its worked example for a fresh cycle totals about $63,500. The donor's compensation is the largest single component: a median of about $10,000, with a range of $8,000 to $20,000, and higher for donors with particular traits — a university degree, a high score on a standardised test, a proven history of donation. The agency fee is $5,000 to $15,000. The recipient's cycle — the medication to prepare her uterus, the transfer itself — is $8,500. Legal fees, for the contract between donor and recipient, are $1,500 to $3,500. Add genetic testing of the embryos at $3,000 to $5,000, and the invoice reaches its total.

The second route is frozen eggs from a bank. Donor Egg Bank USA and Fairfax EggBank sell cohorts of six frozen eggs for about $18,000, or $3,000 an egg, with premium donors at $4,500 an egg, or $27,000 a cohort. The Fertility Link's worked example for a frozen cycle totals about $29,000: $18,000 for the cohort, $8,500 for the recipient's cycle, $2,500 for legal fees, and no agency fee at all, because the bank is the agency. The frozen route is roughly half the price of the fresh route, and it is the route most patients take first. It is also the route with the lower per-egg odds: six frozen eggs do not produce six embryos, and the number of usable embryos from a frozen cohort is typically lower than from a fresh retrieval of the same number of eggs.

The price difference between the two routes is, in the end, a price on certainty. A fresh cycle produces more eggs, more embryos, and more chances in a single attempt. A frozen cohort is cheaper and faster — there is no synchronisation, no waiting for a donor's cycle — but it may require a second cohort. A patient who buys two frozen cohorts has spent $36,000 on eggs alone, and is close to the price of a fresh cycle, with the same number of attempts. The cheapest route is the one that works first.

What the premium buys: the highest success rate in the field

The reason the premium holds is that it works. Donor-egg IVF has the highest live birth rate of any fertility treatment, because the eggs are young. ProIVF, a California clinic, reported a 62 percent single-transfer clinical pregnancy rate for donor-egg cycles in 2024, using its own data. That is roughly double the success rate of a 41-year-old using her own eggs. The premium is not a markup on an identical product. It is a different product, with a different outcome.

The market is also, unusually, transparent about its pricing in one respect and opaque in another. The egg bank prices are published. The agency fees are published. What is not published is the compensation, which is negotiated case by case, and the clinic fees, which vary by geography. ProIVF puts the cost of a donor-egg cycle in California at $25,000 to $45,000, before the egg bank or agency costs, and notes that the total depends on whether the donor is fresh or frozen, known or anonymous, local or travelling. The range is wide enough that two patients at the same clinic can pay meaningfully different prices for the same treatment.

The counterargument: the premium is priced on traits, and that should make you uncomfortable

There is a part of the donor-egg market that the pricing guides describe without comment, and it deserves comment. Donor compensation is not flat. It rises with the donor's education, her test scores, her appearance and her proven fertility. A donor with a degree from an elite university and a history of successful donations commands a higher fee than a donor without them. The market is, in effect, pricing the donor's traits and passing the price to the recipient. The clinics describe this as compensation for time and effort. The compensation scales with traits that have nothing to do with time or effort. That is not a moral judgment. It is an observation about what the price is made of.

There is a second counterargument, and it is about the frozen route. The frozen cohort is cheaper, but the eggs have been frozen, thawed and fertilised, and each step loses some. A cohort of six frozen eggs typically yields two or three usable embryos. A fresh retrieval of fifteen eggs yields more. The frozen route is a good first attempt. It is a poor plan if the patient needs more than one attempt, because the second cohort costs the same as the first and the odds do not improve. The patients who choose frozen eggs for the price and then need two cohorts have paid the fresh-cycle price for the frozen-cycle odds.

There is also the legal and ethical geography. In Canada, paying an egg donor is illegal under the Assisted Human Reproduction Act; donors may be reimbursed only for documented expenses. The American market, by contrast, is a commercial market with negotiated compensation. The same eggs, the same procedure, and a different legal answer on the other side of the border. The price is not a fact of nature. It is a fact of jurisdiction.

What to ask before you pay

Ask the clinic for the price as a single number, all in, for the route you are actually taking — fresh or frozen, with genetic testing or without, with the legal fees included. Ask how many eggs are in the cohort, and how many usable embryos the clinic expects from that number. Ask whether the price includes a second transfer if the first fails, and what the guarantee, if any, covers. Some clinics offer shared-risk programmes for donor-egg cycles, refunding part of the fee if no live birth results. The guarantee is worth reading closely: it usually covers a fixed number of attempts, and it usually excludes the egg bank or agency costs, which are the largest part of the bill.

The donor egg premium is the price of the best odds in fertility medicine. It is worth paying, for the patients who need it. It is also worth pricing honestly, line by line, before the first payment is made. The invoice is complicated because the treatment is complicated. The patient who understands the invoice is the patient who can decide whether the premium is worth it — and whether the cheaper route, taken twice, is cheaper at all.
